the denominator problem

A note on heat tickets, missing rates, and the part of operational intelligence nobody owned.

spark

Another summer, another run of tickets about warm boxes. I wanted to write an essay about it. The framing I started with was that companies now have Palantir-like visibility into their own operations without the institutional capacity to act on it.

question

Why had three summers of accumulating intelligence about a recurring, predictable, expensive failure not produced a fix?

short answer

The premise was wrong. The failure rate went down, substantially, and nobody in the company could see it.

Heat-related tickets per 1,000 regional orders, 1 June to 12 August each year:

summer heat tickets orders rate
2024 26 1,759 14.78
2025 77 4,292 17.94
2026 47 4,343 10.82

Down 40% year over year. But absolute ticket counts went 26, 77, 47, and the queue is what people experience. Everyone felt the system degrading through the exact period it improved.

The reporting layer measured complaints with rising precision for three years and never once computed a rate. There was no denominator anywhere in it. Getting one required joining a ticket export, an order export, and a hand-maintained territory sheet, which nobody had done because no role in the company is defined as the person who joins things.

the other finding

Two shipping zones. One-day zips and two-day zips. Same ice pack count in both. Two-day boxes get a mylar wrap; one-day boxes do not.

  • 1-day, no mylar: 19 heat tickets / 2,427 orders = 7.83 per 1,000
  • 2-day, with mylar: 9 heat tickets / 1,916 orders = 4.70 per 1,000

The zone with less transit time and no wrap failed 1.67 times as often. And 63.6% of regional orders went to one-day zips, so that is where most of the volume sat.

Not proof. Nineteen tickets against nine. The obvious alternative explanation is that one-day zips contain migrated local customers who used to get van delivery and may hold the product to a different standard. But it was enough to justify a controlled test, and it had been invisible.

interesting wrinkles

The field built to catch this was used 11% of the time. A delivery-issue dropdown existed, but was populated on about one ticket in nine. Three years of heat failures were recoverable only by searching free text.

The instrumentation lagged the ownership by a year. Delivery type, zip code, and order number became reliably populated roughly twelve months after someone started owning the problem.

Three systems disagreed by eleven months about when a carrier change happened. Customer text, support fields, order labels, and meeting memory all told different internal stories.

The decision I was investigating left no written trace. The spec reached me as a screenshot of a text message. The zone assignments lived in a spreadsheet. The rationale lived in a long meeting transcript nobody had reason to re-read.

what I got wrong

Worth recording, because the corrections are the actual method.

  1. I tied two operational changes together because the ticket text made it look that way. The dates were not the same.
  2. I built a distance-band analysis on geographic guesses about lane structure. I should have asked for the real schedule first.
  3. I inferred that far-lane boxes were mostly arriving intact from absence, on too little data.
  4. I proposed a weekend-cascade hypothesis that the data did not support.
  5. I accidentally measured field coverage instead of failure rate.
  6. I treated zip-code count as volume. Orders were what mattered.
  7. I called a vendor’s thermal test a lab test when the source did not justify that language.

Every one of these was caught either by pushing back on a number that did not match the floor, or by a new file arriving that had a real denominator in it.

open threads

  • Mylar unit cost, minimum order, and added line labor.
  • The thermal test itself: who ran it, duration in hours, pass threshold, and whether the one-day configuration was ever tested past its design duration.
  • When mylar came off one-day boxes.
  • Carrier scan-level on-time rates by lane.
  • Destination ambient temperature by zip on delivery date.
  • Silent churn: reorder rate after a heat ticket versus baseline.

the thesis it turned into

Not “companies can’t act on what they know.” Closer to this:

Collection and analysis got close to free. Response costs did not move. So the gap widens every year, not because acting got harder but because knowing got cheap. And the thing that stays expensive is not the fix. It is the join. Every piece of the answer existed inside the company, held correctly by a different person in a different system, and none of them was the answer alone.

We automated the report. Nobody automated the part where the report becomes a rate.
